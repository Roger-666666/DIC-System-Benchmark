# What Signals Precede Rock Slope Failure? Precursor Identification with Multiview 3D-XTDIC Displacement Histories

> **Abstract:** A single maximum-displacement threshold is insufficient for interpreting the seismic response of a rock slope. In a large shaking-table test on a physical similarity model, multiview 3D-XTDIC measurements can connect displacement histories at the crest, midslope, and toe with residual deformation, response timing, spatial migration, full-field strain, and marker trajectories. The objective is not a universal numerical trigger, but a traceable combination of evidence showing where differential motion begins and how it evolves.

> **Keywords:** 3D-XTDIC; digital image correlation; rock slope; similarity model; deformation and failure characteristics; full-field strain measurement; rail transit

## The seismic scenario and the limits of a single threshold

When seismic waves with different peak accelerations are applied to a large rock-slope similarity model, the slope does not respond as one rigid body. The crest may develop pronounced cyclic motion, a weak zone near midslope may accumulate differential displacement, and the toe may respond later because of confinement and local loosening. A threshold applied to one maximum value cannot distinguish these mechanisms.

This distinction matters when laboratory observations are discussed in relation to infrastructure corridors, including slopes near rail transit. The useful output of the model is not a number that can be transferred to every site. It is a description of where deformation starts, how it propagates, and whether motion remains after the input ends. Multiview 3D-XTDIC adds spatial context to point histories, allowing a local response to be interpreted within the deformation of the whole visible slope.

### The crest, midslope, and toe tell different parts of the story

Crest displacement can reflect rocking or opening in the upper part of the model. Midslope measurements are more likely to expose relative motion around a weak zone. Toe response is influenced by support, local compression and shear, and the availability of an outward movement path. These histories should therefore be compared rather than ranked independently.

A large crest peak does not prove that sliding started there. Likewise, a delayed toe response does not establish continuing stability. The important questions are whether differences among regions persist, whether residual relative displacement develops, and whether the affected zone expands into a spatially coherent pattern.

### A maximum is an instant, whereas a precursor is a process

A short pulse may generate a high instantaneous displacement without producing a persistent sliding zone. Conversely, a modest peak can accompany repeated increments of residual motion and progressive localization. Treating every large peak as equivalent to imminent failure removes precisely the temporal information needed to interpret the test. The peak remains useful, but only when read together with what happens before and after it.

## Precursors as a combination of features

A defensible precursor description combines amplitude, retained deformation, timing, and location. Increasing input intensity may increase response, but greater motion is not synonymous with a more developed failure mechanism. The analyst must establish whether deformation becomes localized and whether that localization persists across successive stages of the record.

### Separate peak displacement from residual displacement

Peak displacement describes response during shaking. Residual displacement describes the relative change that remains after the relevant input or interval. If several key points reach large peaks together and then return approximately to their previous configuration, the observation alone does not demonstrate a connected sliding zone. If midslope relative displacement is retained while neighboring points show a consistent displacement gradient, the weak zone deserves closer examination.

This is a comparison of trends and spatial relations, not an attempt to assign a universal critical displacement. Reference states also matter: residual motion should be evaluated against a clearly defined stage of the test rather than inferred from unrelated images.

### Timing reveals organization, but not causation by itself

The order of peaks, changes in slope of the displacement-time curves, and the sequence in which residual motion appears can reveal how deformation is organized. An anomaly may first be visible in the upper slope, then extend toward midslope while adjacent views begin to show compatible differential movement. Such a sequence is stronger evidence than an isolated peak.

Timing still requires caution. Apparent delays can arise from wave propagation, structural dynamics, sampling alignment, or the selected image-processing interval. Arriving first at a peak is therefore not equivalent to failing first. Time differences become informative when they recur and correspond to spatial changes.

### Spatial migration and multiview agreement reduce ambiguity

A displacement anomaly in the front view gains support when side-view strain localization or marker trajectories show a compatible development in location and time. In contrast, a patch that appears in only one frame, changes position erratically, and lacks neighboring support should prompt checks of speckle quality, occlusion, correlation, and camera stability. Multiview agreement is not a decorative confirmation; it is a way to reject interpretations that depend on one projection.

| Stage of the record | What to compare in displacement histories | What to inspect in spatial fields | Cautious interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial input | Onset and peak order at crest, midslope, and toe | Whether anomalies are isolated or spatially continuous | Separate broad dynamic response from early differential motion |
| Increasing response | Peak-to-peak retention and growing regional differences | Persistent concentration near a weak zone | Identify accumulating deformation without relying on amplitude alone |
| Anomaly development | Changes in curve slope and relative timing | Migration, lengthening, or connection of anomalous zones | Discuss organization of sliding and the completeness of evidence |
| Interval or end of input | Whether residual displacement remains | Agreement among cracks, strain concentration, and marker paths | Distinguish recoverable vibration from retained deformation |
| Multiview review | Common timing of key events | Compatibility among different projections | Retain supported findings and mark unresolved areas |

## Evidence from the public test description

The public case uses a two-sided camera arrangement. The front view measures full-field displacement, the side view supports full-field strain analysis, and markers on the model surface assist local tracking. These observations serve different purposes: displacement fields show how regions move, strain fields show where deformation concentrates, and marker paths provide an additional description of selected surface locations.

### Front-view displacement establishes regional contrasts

Multiview displacement histories obtained under different seismic inputs allow key points at the crest, midslope, and toe to be compared in amplitude, timing, and retained response. The full field also supplies the neighborhood missing from an isolated curve. It can show whether a point belongs to a continuous deforming region or behaves differently from its surroundings. On this basis, the earliest deforming weak zone can be identified and its subsequent expansion examined.

The comparison should remain tied to the observed surface and viewing direction. A front-view displacement component does not automatically describe out-of-plane sliding, and a visually prominent band should not be declared a failure surface without supporting temporal and spatial evidence.

### Side-view strain and markers provide independent checks

Side-view strain maps can be examined for concentrations that develop consistently with front-view displacement anomalies. Markers add trajectories that are easy to follow across critical intervals. Neither observation is a complete substitute for the other. Strain is derived from spatial displacement gradients and can be sensitive to local processing choices, while markers describe only their immediate locations.

The value of the arrangement lies in convergence among different forms of evidence. Where the views disagree, the disagreement should remain visible in the interpretation rather than being hidden by selective presentation.

## A multiview measurement strategy

The imaging geometry must remain stable throughout shaking. Fixing a camera means more than leaving its body in place: the support, optical axis, and field of view must not introduce apparent motion. If the camera moves with the table, measured displacement may contain a common rigid component. If views are not aligned in time, the order of events can be misread.

### Distinguish model deformation from reference-region vibration

A reference region that follows the model support or another appropriate frame can help estimate common translation, rotation, or imaging drift. It must not lie inside the sliding zone being investigated, or the correction may remove the deformation of interest. Table input, whole-model motion, and relative deformation within the slope should be treated as separate concepts.

This separation is particularly important for residual displacement. A common offset across the visible model may reflect frame motion, whereas a spatially varying offset concentrated near a weak zone has a different mechanical meaning. The correction procedure should be stated with the result so that the origin of each displacement component remains clear.

### Organize comparisons around events rather than peak images

For tests with different peak acceleration inputs, useful events include the onset of response, establishment of noticeable residual displacement, and expansion of an anomalous zone. Organizing the analysis around these events retains the sequence that a collection of maximum-value maps would lose.

Digital image correlation measures surface displacement relative to a reference image or stage. Reference selection, tracking continuity through occlusion, and time bases across views therefore affect interpretation. Regions with unreliable correlation should remain blank or explicitly marked as low confidence. Interpolating them into a continuous band would create visual certainty that the measurements do not support.

## Model limits and the next question

A similarity model reproduces deformation and failure behavior under particular assumptions about geometry, materials, boundaries, and input motion. Its conclusions cannot be transferred directly to a field slope. Scale effects, discontinuity distributions, drainage, support conditions, and earthquake characteristics may all alter the response. The public case does not establish a general warning threshold, and no critical acceleration value is needed for the precursor analysis presented here.

The more useful conclusion is methodological: precursor identification becomes stronger when growing regional contrasts, accumulated residual displacement, spatial migration, and multiview support are observed together. Once the earliest weak zone has been located, the next task is to examine how strain localization develops, whether a band connects into a sliding zone, and how these sequences differ among failure modes.

## FAQ

### Why is maximum displacement insufficient as a warning indicator?

It does not distinguish recoverable vibration from retained deformation, identify the initiating region, or show whether an anomaly is expanding. Timing and spatial context are also required.

### Does the earliest crest peak mean that failure starts at the crest?

No. Wave propagation and dynamic response can change peak timing. Initiation should be assessed using relative motion, spatial development, and supporting observations from other views.

### Can marker tracking replace full-field measurement?

They serve different roles. Markers provide selected trajectories, while full-field displacement and strain reveal spatial continuity and neighboring behavior. Their combination is more informative than either alone.

### Can model-derived precursors be applied directly in the field?

Not directly. The model supports mechanism-based interpretation, but field use requires site geology, appropriate scaling considerations, and independent monitoring evidence.
